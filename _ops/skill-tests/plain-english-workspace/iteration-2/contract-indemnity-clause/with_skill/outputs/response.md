**The short version**
Yes, as written it's bad for you. You'd be agreeing to pay for any loss the client has that's connected to your work. That includes their legal bills, and it includes losses that are partly their own fault. There's no limit on the amount, and it carries on after the contract ends. This is the clause to push back on before you sign.

**In plain English**
- **"Indemnify and hold harmless"** means you agree to cover their costs if something goes wrong, and you won't come after them for it. Their staff and contractors are covered too.
- **"Any and all losses… arising out of or in connection with the Services"** is very wide. It isn't limited to you doing a bad job. It covers anything with a link to your work. Say a campaign gets them a complaint, a platform bans their ad account, or a supplier of theirs sues over an image. They could hand you the bill.
- **"Howsoever caused, including by negligence"** means it doesn't matter who caused the problem. Read on its own, "negligence" could even include theirs.
- **"Save to the extent caused by the Client's wilful misconduct"** is the only way out. You're off the hook only for the part they caused deliberately. If they were just careless, say they approved the ad or gave you the wrong information, you still pay.
- **"Legal costs on a solicitor-client basis"** means their full lawyer's bill. When you lose a case in court you normally pay only part of the other side's legal costs. This clause makes you pay close to all of it.
- **"Uncapped"** means there's no upper limit. It isn't capped at your fees, at your insurance, or at anything else.
- **"Survives termination"** means it keeps going after the contract ends. A claim could turn up years later.

One thing that catches people out: your professional indemnity insurance (the cover for mistakes in your work) usually won't pay for liability you've taken on by contract beyond what you'd owe under the law anyway. So a clause like this can leave you covering the gap yourself.

**Think of it like this**
It's like a plumber signing a job sheet that says they'll pay for any water damage in the house from now on, whoever caused it. That includes the homeowner forgetting to turn off a tap. There's no dollar limit, and it applies long after the plumber has packed up the van. The only exception is if the homeowner floods the place on purpose. A normal job sheet says the plumber pays for leaks from their own work, up to a sensible amount.

**What you need to do**
Don't sign it as is. Ask for these changes. They're standard and most clients will accept some or all of them:

1. **Add a cap.** Limit your total liability to the fees they've paid you in the last 12 months, or to your insurance cover, whichever is lower.
2. **Only cover what you cause.** Limit it to losses caused by your breach of the contract, or by your (or your staff's) negligence or wrongdoing.
3. **Reduce it when they contribute.** Your share goes down to the extent the client, or anyone working for them, caused or contributed to the loss.
4. **Rule out knock-on losses.** Exclude "indirect or consequential loss", meaning lost profits, lost revenue and lost data.
5. **Make it mutual.** They cover you the same way for things they cause, like content they supplied or approvals they gave.
6. **Drop "solicitor-client basis"**, or have it apply only up to the cap.

Before you go back to them, send it to your insurance broker and ask, "Would my PI policy cover this clause as written?" Then get a commercial lawyer to look over the whole contract. It should be a quick, cheap job, and a clause like this is often a sign there are others like it. This is a general explanation, not legal advice.
