**The short version**
The business now earns more than $75,000 a year, so the law says you have to start charging GST (the 10% tax added to most sales) and pass it on to the tax office every three months. Your accountant is telling you to sign up, change your invoices, keep your receipts, and decide one thing: what date all this starts from.

**In plain English**
Here's the order it happens in:

1. **Decide your start date (you, now).** This is the "EDR" (effective date of registration), the day GST officially starts applying to you. Your accountant is asking whether to set it earlier than today ("backdate" it). The catch: if it's backdated, you owe GST on sales since that date, even though you never charged clients for it. That money comes out of your pocket unless you go back and bill clients for it. Ask the accountant why they're suggesting it before you say yes.

2. **Register for GST (the accountant or you, within 21 days).** The 21-day clock started when you went over $75k, so it may already be running. Your accountant can do this for you, which is the easiest option.

3. **Sort out your prices and invoices (you, from the start date).** Every invoice from that date has to say "Tax invoice", show your ABN, and show the GST amount. You also need to decide whether you add 10% on top of your current prices or keep prices the same and absorb it. For retainer clients, it's worth telling them before the first new invoice lands.

4. **Keep receipts for business purchases (you, ongoing).** Any business purchase over $82.50 needs a proper tax invoice from the supplier. That's how you claim back the GST you paid on things like software, ads and equipment. No proper receipt, no claim.

5. **Report every quarter (the accountant, every three months).** This is the BAS (business activity statement), a quarterly form for the tax office. Box 1A is the GST you charged clients. Box 1B is the GST you paid on business costs. You pay the tax office the difference. Your first one covers October to December and is due 28 February, when the accountant lodges it for you.

6. **Possibly prepay income tax (only if the tax office says so).** "PAYG instalments" means paying your income tax in quarterly chunks on the same form, instead of one big bill at the end of the year. The tax office will tell you if it applies. It's not a new tax, just paying the same tax earlier.

**Where people get caught out:** "Accruals basis" means GST counts from the day you send an invoice, not the day the client pays. So if a client pays late, you may owe the tax office GST you haven't received yet. Many small businesses can choose to report when they get paid instead ("cash basis"), so ask your accountant if that's an option for you. The other trap is spending the GST. It was never your money, so put it aside as it comes in.

**Think of it like this**
You've just been handed a jar that belongs to the tax office. Every time you invoice a client, 10% goes into the jar. Every time you pay GST on a business cost, you can take that amount back out, as long as you've got the receipt. Each quarter, the BAS is you counting the jar and handing over what's left. The one difference from a real jar: on accruals, money goes in when you send the invoice, even if the client hasn't paid yet.

**What you need to do**
- Reply to the accountant: "Please register me for GST. Why would you backdate the start date, and what would it cost me?" Also ask: "Can I report on a cash basis instead of accruals?"
- Decide whether your prices go up 10% or stay the same, and tell retainer clients before the change.
- Update your invoice template: "Tax invoice", your ABN, and the GST amount shown separately.
- Start keeping receipts for business purchases over $82.50.
- Set up a separate account and move the GST into it each time a client pays.
