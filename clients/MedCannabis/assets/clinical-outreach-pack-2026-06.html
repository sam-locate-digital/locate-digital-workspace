Why this is the #1 acquisition channel

The GP knowledge gap is your growth engine

52%
of Australian GPs feel comfortable discussing medicinal cannabis with patients (2021–22 survey)
85%
of GPs have received a patient enquiry about medicinal cannabis in the past three months
5,284
registered prescribers in Australia as of 2022 — many still rely on specialist clinics to manage their cases

Almost every GP is getting patient questions about medicinal cannabis. Less than half know what to say. The ones who feel comfortable either refer to a telehealth service or write scripts themselves — but most don't write scripts, because they're unsure of the clinical pathway, the evidence base, or what to prescribe.

That's an opening. A clinic that positions itself as the GP's trusted clinical partner — not a competitor, not a threat to the patient relationship — can build a referral pipeline in months, not years. The telehealth giants cannot do this. They have no local presence, no face, and no relationship with the GPs in your postcode.

One GP who actively refers sends two to five patients a month. Ten active GP referrers generates 20–50 new patients every month from a single channel, with essentially zero ongoing cost.

Referral targets

Who to approach and in what order

Priority 1

GPs within 5 km

Local GPs are the fastest path to referrals. They want to refer patients somewhere nearby, not to a website. Start here.

  • Solo GP practices
  • Group practices with chronic disease focus
  • GPs with pain management patients
  • GPs who already see mental health patients
Priority 2

Pain specialists and physios

Chronic pain is the #1 prescribing category. Specialists who manage refractory pain see patients who have exhausted other options.

  • Rheumatologists
  • Anaesthetists with pain clinic
  • Physiotherapists and osteopaths
  • Musculoskeletal specialists
Priority 3

Mental health specialists

Psychiatrists treating PTSD, anxiety, and treatment-resistant depression are increasingly open to medicinal cannabis as an adjunct.

  • Psychiatrists
  • Psychologists (refer, can't prescribe)
  • Mental health GPs
  • PTSD-focused practices

Neurologists

Epilepsy and MS patients are a smaller but high-need cohort. Neurologists are typically more conservative — relationship-building matters more here.

  • Neurologists with epilepsy patients
  • MS nurses and coordinators
  • Parkinson's disease specialists

Oncology and palliative care

Oncologists and palliative care teams manage patients with complex symptom burdens. Cannabis for nausea, pain, and appetite is well-accepted clinically.

  • Oncologists at local hospitals
  • Palliative care nurses
  • Hospital social workers
  • Community palliative care services

Allied health networks

Allied health practitioners can't prescribe but they refer patients to GPs and specialists daily. Being on their radar matters.

  • Occupational therapists
  • Exercise physiologists
  • Naturopaths and integrative practitioners
  • Community health centres
Outreach programme

Three phases, 12 months

Phase 1 — Months 1–3: Foundation

Map the local referral landscape and make first contact

Before anything else, build the target list. This is desk research — find every GP practice, pain clinic, and mental health practice within 5–10 km. Rate them by patient profile match.

  • Build a spreadsheet of 50–80 target practices with address, principal GP name, and phone
  • Send introductory letters to all 50–80 targets (template below)
  • Follow up by phone within two weeks to offer a brief clinic visit or info drop-off
  • Host a first lunch-and-learn at the clinic for 5–10 local GPs (cover basic clinical education)
  • Prepare printed GP information sheet (leave-behind) — design version below
Phase 2 — Months 4–6: Relationship building

Convert interested GPs into active referrers

The GPs who responded to phase one outreach need nurturing. Regular, low-friction touchpoints keep the clinic top of mind when the next relevant patient comes in.

  • Visit the top 10–15 practices in person — 15-minute "pop-in" with a small gift and a brochure refresh
  • Send a monthly clinical update email to all contacts (not marketing — clinical, 200 words, one useful stat or study finding)
  • Host a second lunch-and-learn, this time condition-specific (e.g. chronic pain and cannabis: the evidence)
  • Set up a GP hotline or direct contact number for clinical questions
  • Send a referral pathway document confirming how to refer and what happens next (template below)
Phase 3 — Months 7–12: Network maintenance

Sustain the relationships and expand the network

Active referrers need recognition and continued contact. Cold targets from phase one who haven't responded yet may be ready now — circumstances change.

  • Thank active referrers with a quarterly clinical update letter (with anonymised case-type stats from the clinic)
  • Expand outreach to the next ring — practices 10–20 km from the clinic
  • Attend local GP and specialist CME events — visibility without a hard sell
  • Re-contact phase one non-responders with a brief "just checking in" email
  • Explore specialist hospital outreach — pain clinics, oncology units, neurology departments

This email should feel like a clinical colleague update, not a newsletter. Never use marketing language. One study, one clinical observation, one referral reminder. That's it.
GP objection handling

What GPs say and what to say back

"I'm not sure the evidence base is strong enough."
That's a reasonable position, and the evidence varies a lot by condition. For chronic neuropathic pain, there's RCT-level evidence. For PTSD-related sleep, the evidence is solid. For general anxiety, it's emerging. We're not asking you to endorse it as a first-line treatment — we're asking you to refer patients who haven't responded to first or second-line options and want to explore it. The patients are going to try it regardless; the question is whether they do it with or without clinical oversight.
"I'm worried about addiction or dependence risk."
That's worth taking seriously. CBD-dominant products have no meaningful addiction risk. THC-containing products do carry dependence potential, particularly in younger patients and those with a personal or family history of cannabis use disorder. We screen for these factors before prescribing and we don't prescribe THC-dominant products to patients with those risk factors. We'd be happy to share our screening protocol if that's useful.
"I have patients who've tried the big telehealth services and had a bad experience."
We understand why that happens. The large telehealth clinics operate a volume model — short consultations, limited follow-up, products chosen from a supplier-affiliated range. We operate differently. Consultations are longer, follow-up care is coordinated back to you, and we prescribe from across the full market, not from a commercial partner's catalogue. If a patient came to us after a bad experience elsewhere, we'd take the time to start properly.
"I don't want to lose the patient relationship."
You won't. We position ourselves as a specialist service you refer to, not a clinic that replaces you. We send clinical summaries after each appointment. For ongoing patients, we recommend annual reviews with you for their broader health management. The patient stays yours — we manage the cannabis prescribing specifically.
"What's the regulatory pathway? I don't understand TGA and SAS-B."
That's actually why referral to us makes sense. We handle the TGA SAS-B application process entirely. For most conditions, you don't need to do anything except refer the patient to us. If you want to become an Authorised Prescriber and manage cannabis yourself, we can walk you through that process too — it's not complicated once you know the steps.
