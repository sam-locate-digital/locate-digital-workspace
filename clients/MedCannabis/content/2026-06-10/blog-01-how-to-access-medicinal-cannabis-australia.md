# How to access medicinal cannabis in Australia: the complete guide

Medicinal cannabis has been legal in Australia since 2016, but the access process still confuses a lot of people. Some assume they can walk into a dispensary and buy it. Others assume it's nearly impossible to get. Neither is true.

This guide explains how access actually works, who can prescribe, what the appointment involves, and what it costs. It's written for patients considering medicinal cannabis for the first time.

## The short version

Medicinal cannabis is a prescription medicine. You can't buy it over the counter, and you can't legally order it online without a script. To access it, you need a consultation with a doctor, an assessment of whether it suits your condition, and a prescription dispensed through a pharmacy or licensed dispensary.

The whole process can take as little as a week. For many patients it's faster than they expect.

## Who can prescribe medicinal cannabis?

Any registered doctor in Australia can prescribe medicinal cannabis. They don't need to be a specialist. In practice, prescriptions come through two pathways set up by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA):

**The Special Access Scheme (SAS-B).** The doctor applies to the TGA for approval to prescribe an unapproved medicine for a specific patient. Approvals usually come back within a couple of business days, and many are processed within 48 hours.

**Authorised Prescribers.** These doctors hold standing TGA approval to prescribe certain medicinal cannabis products to patients under their care, without applying for each patient individually. This makes the process quicker.

Some GPs are comfortable prescribing. Many aren't, simply because it sits outside their usual practice. If your regular GP doesn't prescribe, you can ask for a referral or book directly with a clinic that has prescribers on staff, either in person or by telehealth.

## Who is eligible?

There's no fixed list of approved conditions, but prescribers generally look for two things:

1. **A chronic condition.** Most prescribers want to see a condition that has lasted three months or longer. Common examples include chronic pain, anxiety, PTSD, sleep disorders, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, and symptoms related to cancer treatment or palliative care.
2. **Previous treatments that haven't worked well enough.** Medicinal cannabis is not a first-line treatment. Doctors will ask what you've already tried, whether that's medication, physiotherapy, psychology, or something else, and how it went.

Eligibility is a clinical judgement, not a checkbox exercise. A doctor may decide medicinal cannabis isn't appropriate for you, particularly if you have a history of psychosis, certain heart conditions, or are pregnant or breastfeeding.

## What happens at the consultation

The first appointment is a standard medical consultation. Expect the doctor to ask about:

- Your condition and how long you've had it
- Treatments you've tried and how you responded
- Your general health, current medications, and medical history
- Your work, driving needs, and lifestyle (this matters, more on that below)

Bring whatever records you have. A referral or health summary from your GP helps, but most clinics can work without one.

If the doctor decides medicinal cannabis is clinically appropriate, they'll talk you through product types, what to expect, possible side effects, and the legal considerations. Then they apply for TGA approval (if needed) and write the prescription.

## Filling the prescription

Once approved, your prescription is dispensed like any other medicine: through a pharmacy or a licensed dispensary. Depending on the provider, you can collect in person or have it delivered. Delivery requires ID verification because most medicinal cannabis products are Schedule 8 controlled medicines.

## What it costs

Medicinal cannabis is not subsidised under the PBS, so patients pay the full cost. Budget for:

- **Consultation fees.** These vary by clinic. Some bulk bill the initial assessment; most charge a private fee.
- **The medicine itself.** Costs vary widely depending on the product and dose your doctor prescribes. Your prescriber can give you a realistic monthly estimate before you commit.
- **Follow-up appointments.** Prescribers are required to review your progress, usually within the first month and then periodically.

If cost is a concern, raise it at the consultation. It's a normal part of the conversation.

## Driving and work: know the rules before you start

This is the part patients most often miss. THC, the psychoactive component in many medicinal cannabis products, can be detected in roadside drug tests, and in most Australian states it is an offence to drive with any THC in your system, even with a valid prescription. The rules differ between states and are changing, so ask your doctor about the current law where you live before you start treatment.

Workplaces with drug testing policies are a similar story. If your job involves testing, tell your prescriber. It will shape what they recommend.

## How long does it all take?

For most patients: one to two weeks from booking the first appointment to having medicine in hand. Telehealth has made the process much faster than it was a few years ago, and same-week appointments are common.

## Where to start

Talk to a doctor. That can be your regular GP, or a clinic with prescribers on staff. The consultation is an assessment, not a guarantee of a prescription, and that's how it should be. A prescriber who says yes to everyone isn't doing their job.

*Medicinal cannabis is a prescription medicine. Speak to a doctor to find out if it's right for you.*
