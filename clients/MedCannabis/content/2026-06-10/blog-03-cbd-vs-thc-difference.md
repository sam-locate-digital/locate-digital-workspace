If you've started reading about medicinal cannabis, you've run into these two abbreviations within about thirty seconds. CBD and THC are the two main active compounds in medicinal cannabis, and the difference between them shapes everything: how a medicine affects you, how it's regulated, and what your doctor considers when prescribing.

Here's what each one is, how they differ, and why the choice between them is a clinical decision rather than a shopping decision.

## Two compounds, one plant

The cannabis plant contains more than 100 naturally occurring compounds called cannabinoids. Two of them dominate both the research and the prescriptions:

**THC (tetrahydrocannabinol)** is the psychoactive compound. It's responsible for the "high" associated with recreational cannabis, though at prescribed medical doses the goal is symptom relief, not intoxication. THC interacts strongly with receptors in the brain and nervous system that influence pain perception, appetite, and sleep.

**CBD (cannabidiol)** is not intoxicating. It doesn't produce a high at any dose. It interacts with the body's systems differently and more indirectly than THC, and it's the compound behind most of the research into epilepsy, anxiety, and inflammation.

Prescription products contain THC, CBD, or both in a defined ratio. The ratio is one of the main things a prescriber considers when matching a medicine to a patient.

## How they're regulated in Australia

The legal treatment of the two compounds reflects the difference in their effects.

**THC products are Schedule 8 controlled medicines**, the same category as opioids and other medicines with potential for misuse. Prescribing involves additional TGA and state-level requirements, and dispensing rules are stricter.

**CBD-only products are Schedule 4 prescription medicines**, the same category as antibiotics or blood pressure medication. Still prescription-only, but with fewer controls around prescribing and dispensing.

Either way, you need a prescription. Low-dose CBD was technically rescheduled to allow over-the-counter pharmacy sale in 2021, but no product has yet completed the TGA approval process required to actually be sold that way. Anything claiming otherwise, particularly products sold online without a script, is operating outside the regulated system, and you have no guarantee of what's actually in the bottle.

## The practical differences for patients

**Intoxication.** THC can impair you, particularly when starting treatment or changing dose. CBD doesn't. This affects what you can safely do after taking your medicine, and it's why prescribers often start cautiously with THC-containing products.

**Driving.** This is the big one. In most Australian states it's an offence to drive with any detectable THC in your system, even with a valid prescription, and THC can be detected well after its effects have worn off. CBD is not tested for in roadside drug tests, and CBD-only products generally don't carry the same driving restrictions. If you drive for work or family, tell your doctor, because it directly affects what they can responsibly prescribe. Laws differ between states and are under review in several, so get current advice for where you live.

**Workplace drug testing.** Same logic. Workplace tests target THC. If your job involves testing, your prescriber needs to know.

**Side effects.** Both compounds have them. THC's are more noticeable: drowsiness, dizziness, dry mouth, and at higher doses, anxiety or confusion in some people. CBD is generally well tolerated, with fatigue and digestive upset the most commonly reported issues. CBD can also interact with other medications, including some blood thinners and anti-seizure medicines, which is one reason "it's natural" doesn't mean "it's harmless."

## Why "which one is right for me?" is your doctor's question to answer

It's tempting to read a few articles and arrive at the consultation having already picked. Patients do it all the time. But the choice between THC, CBD, or a combination depends on factors you can't fully weigh yourself:

- The condition being treated, and what the evidence says works for it
- Your other medications and how they interact
- Your medical history, including mental health history
- Your age, and how your body processes medicines
- Whether you drive, and what your job requires
- How you respond once treatment starts, which nobody can predict in advance

Prescribers typically start conservatively and adjust based on your response and side effects. That process, trialling, reviewing, and adjusting under supervision, is the actual answer to "which is right for me." It's not something a blog post can tell you, including this one.

## The bottom line

THC and CBD come from the same plant but behave differently in the body and the law. THC is psychoactive, tightly controlled, and carries driving and workplace implications. CBD is non-intoxicating and more lightly regulated, but still prescription-only in any meaningful dose. Most prescribing decisions come down to matching the compound, ratio, and dose to your condition and circumstances, and that's a decision you make with a doctor, not a search engine.

*Medicinal cannabis is a prescription medicine. Speak to a doctor to find out if it's right for you.*
