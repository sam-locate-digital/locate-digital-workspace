# Medicinal cannabis and chronic pain: what the evidence says

**Pillar:** Chronic pain

Chronic pain is the most common reason Australians seek a medicinal cannabis prescription. It's also the area where patients arrive with the biggest expectations, shaped by headlines, forums, and the experiences of friends.

So it's worth being honest about what the research actually shows. The evidence for medicinal cannabis in chronic pain is real but mixed, and anyone who tells you it works for everyone is overselling it. Here's a plain reading of where things stand.

## What we mean by chronic pain

Chronic pain is pain that persists for three months or longer. It affects around one in five Australians over 45, and it isn't one condition. It includes:

- **Neuropathic pain**, caused by nerve damage or dysfunction, such as sciatica, diabetic neuropathy, or pain after shingles
- **Musculoskeletal pain**, including back pain, arthritis, and joint conditions
- **Fibromyalgia**, a condition involving widespread pain and fatigue
- **Cancer-related pain**, from the disease itself or its treatment

The distinction matters, because the evidence for medicinal cannabis differs depending on the type of pain.

## What the research shows

The most studied area is neuropathic pain. Several systematic reviews have found that cannabis-based medicines can produce modest reductions in neuropathic pain for some patients compared with placebo. "Modest" is the honest word. The effect is meaningful for the patients who respond, but not everyone responds, and the average improvement across studies is small to moderate.

For other types of chronic pain, the picture is less clear. Trials in musculoskeletal pain and fibromyalgia are fewer, smaller, and have produced inconsistent results. Some patients in these studies report improvements in pain, sleep, and quality of life. Others see no benefit over placebo.

A few things make the research hard to interpret:

- Many trials are short, often 12 weeks or less, while chronic pain is a long-term condition
- Studies use different products, doses, and ratios of active compounds, so results don't transfer neatly from one to another
- Pain is subjective, and placebo effects in pain trials are consistently large

Australia's peak pain bodies have taken a cautious position for these reasons. The Faculty of Pain Medicine, for instance, does not recommend cannabinoids as a first-line treatment for chronic non-cancer pain. That caution is reasonable, and patients deserve to know it exists.

## Why do doctors prescribe it, then?

Because chronic pain is difficult to treat, and the existing options have problems of their own.

Many patients arrive at a medicinal cannabis consultation after years of trying conventional treatments. Anti-inflammatories carry gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks with long-term use. Opioids carry tolerance and dependence risks, and Australia has spent the past decade trying to reduce long-term opioid prescribing. Physiotherapy, pain programs, and psychology help many people but not all.

For a patient who has genuinely worked through those options and still lives with daily pain, a trial of medicinal cannabis under medical supervision can be a reasonable next step. That's the standard the TGA's access pathways are built around: it's not a first resort, it's a considered option when first-line treatments haven't done enough.

## What a responsible trial looks like

If a doctor prescribes medicinal cannabis for chronic pain, the process should look like any other medical trial of a new treatment:

1. **A baseline.** Your doctor records your current pain levels, sleep, function, and mood, so there's something to measure against.
2. **A defined trial period.** Usually weeks to a few months, with a follow-up review booked before you start.
3. **An honest review.** If it's helping, treatment continues with periodic reviews. If it isn't, the prescription stops. Continuing a medicine that isn't working is bad practice, whatever the medicine is.

Side effects are part of the conversation too. The most common ones reported in trials include drowsiness, dizziness, dry mouth, and changes in appetite or mood. Most are mild and dose-related, but they're not nothing, and your doctor should explain them before you start.

## Questions worth asking your doctor

If you're considering medicinal cannabis for chronic pain, these questions will get you a useful conversation:

- Based on my type of pain, what does the evidence say about my likelihood of benefit?
- What would we measure to decide if it's working?
- How does this interact with my current medications?
- What are the implications for my driving and my job?
- What's the plan if it doesn't help?

A good prescriber will welcome every one of these.

## The bottom line

Medicinal cannabis is not a cure for chronic pain, and the evidence doesn't support prescribing it to everyone. It is a legitimate, legal treatment option that helps some patients, particularly some of those with neuropathic pain, when conventional treatments haven't delivered enough relief. The only way to know whether you're one of them is a proper medical assessment, a supervised trial, and an honest review.

*Medicinal cannabis is a prescription medicine. Speak to a doctor to find out if it's right for you.*
