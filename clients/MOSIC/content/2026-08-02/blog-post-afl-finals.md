# Footy finals in Melbourne: why weekend players end up at the osteopath

Local footy finals are a few weeks out, and across Melbourne's amateur leagues the intensity has already stepped up. Grounds from Elsternwick to Essendon are heavy with winter rain, the contests are harder, and a lot of the blokes lining up on Saturday have spent the week sitting at a desk in the CBD.

That combination is where the trouble starts. A body that's been in a chair from Monday to Friday gets asked to sprint, turn, tackle and land on a wet ground at full noise, often while carrying a niggle that's been there since round twelve.

The Bourke Street clinic sees a run of the same injuries every August and September. Most of them are the kind that were preventable a fortnight earlier, and treatable in a way that gets a player to the finish of the season rather than watching it from the boundary.

## The injuries that spike at finals time

Finals footy loads the body harder than the home-and-away season, and it does it on players who are already worn down from a long winter.

Hamstring strains are the most common. Explosive sprinting off a standing start puts enormous load through the hamstring, and a cold, fatigued muscle on a heavy ground is far more likely to tear. Most footy hamstrings go in the first or last quarter, when the muscle is either not warm or already spent.

Corked thighs and calf strains come next. A cork is a direct blow that crushes the muscle against the bone, and players tend to run them off and pay for it the next day. Calf strains climb late in the season as accumulated fatigue and heavy grounds ask more of the lower leg.

Then the joints. Rolled ankles are constant on wet, chopped-up surfaces. AC joint sprains and shoulder knocks come from tackles, bumps and awkward falls. Knees take a beating too, with medial ligament strains from the classic sidestep that doesn't quite come off on a slippery deck.

The through-line is fatigue and load. Players who've pushed through a niggle all year, who train once instead of twice, and who sit at a desk all week are the ones who end up injured when the pace lifts for finals.

## How osteopathy helps a footy injury

The assessment at the Bourke Street or Essendon clinic starts with working out exactly what's been injured and how badly, because that changes everything about the plan.

A hamstring strain, for instance, is graded on how much of the muscle is involved. A minor grade one might need a fortnight and the right loading. A grade two needs longer and a proper return-to-run progression before match pace. Guessing at that, or rushing it for a big game, is how a two-week injury becomes a two-month one.

Treatment addresses the injured tissue and the reason it went in the first place. For a hamstring, MOSIC's practitioners look at hip mobility, pelvic position and how the glutes are firing, because a hamstring often tears because it's compensating for something further up the chain that isn't doing its job. Hands-on work through the injured muscle is paired with the joints and structures around it.

For joint injuries, an ankle or an AC joint, the priority is settling the acute phase, restoring movement, and rebuilding stability before the player goes back into contact. Where an injury needs a scan or a review, it gets referred on with a clear reason why, not left to guesswork.

## What you can do to get through finals

The players who finish the season standing up tend to do a few unglamorous things well.

Warm up like you mean it. A cold hamstring is a hamstring waiting to tear. Ten minutes of rising-intensity running, some leg swings and a few build-up sprints before the first bounce does more to protect you than any amount of static stretching. This matters most in the first quarter, when most soft-tissue injuries happen.

Respect the niggle. The tightness that's been there for three weeks is information. Pushing through it week after week is the surest way to turn a manageable strain into a full tear right when you can least afford it. Get it looked at early and you usually keep playing.

Recover between games. A single weekly session plus a Saturday match, on a body that sits all week, doesn't leave much conditioning in the tank. Light movement between games, a proper cool-down, and actual sleep do more for recovery than an ice bath posted to Instagram.

Load the calves and hamstrings through the week. Simple calf raises and hamstring bridges, done a couple of times a week, build the resilience that heavy grounds demand. It's dull, and it works.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I see an osteopath in Melbourne CBD for a footy injury without a referral?**

**What's the difference between an osteopath and a physio for a footy injury?**

There's real overlap, and both treat sports injuries well. Osteopaths tend to assess the whole movement chain, so for a hamstring they'll look at hip mobility, pelvic position and glute function rather than treating only the sore spot. For a recurring soft-tissue injury that keeps coming back, that broader assessment often finds the driver that's been missed.

**How many sessions will a hamstring strain take?**

It depends on the grade. A minor strain caught early might settle in two or three sessions alongside the right loading. A more significant tear takes longer, often four to six sessions with a staged return to running before you're back at match pace. Your practitioner will give you a realistic timeline after assessing it, including whether it's safe to play finals.

**Can a corked thigh or a footy injury cause problems elsewhere?**

Yes. When you protect one leg, you overload the other, and the change in the way you move can throw load onto the lower back, hip or opposite hamstring. It's common to injure one thing on the weekend and turn up a week later with something sore on the other side. Treating the whole pattern, not just the original injury, is what stops that happening.

**Is osteopathic treatment for a sports injury covered by private health?**

Most extras cover includes a rebate for osteopathy, and a footy injury is covered the same as any musculoskeletal complaint. The amount depends on your fund and level of cover. If the injury happened at work or in a car, WorkCover and TAC claims are accepted. MOSIC's reception can confirm what applies when you book.
