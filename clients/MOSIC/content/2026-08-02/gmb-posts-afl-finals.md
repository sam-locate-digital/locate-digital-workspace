GMB POST 1: ~2 August

Common footy finals injuries 🏉

Finals footy loads the body harder than the regular season, and it does it on players who are already worn down from a long winter. Heavy grounds and higher intensity are behind the spike in injuries we see every August and September.

The ones that turn up most:
- Hamstring strains: explosive sprinting off a standing start puts big load through the hamstring. A cold or fatigued muscle on a heavy ground tears more easily, usually in the first or last quarter
- Corked thighs: a direct blow that crushes the muscle against the bone. Players run them off and feel it the next day
- Calf strains: climb late in the season as fatigue and wet grounds ask more of the lower leg
- Rolled ankles: constant on chopped-up winter surfaces
- AC joint and shoulder knocks: from tackles, bumps and awkward falls

The common thread is fatigue and load. A niggle carried all season, one training run instead of two, and a desk job through the week is the profile that ends up injured when the pace lifts.

Pain that follows a game is worth getting looked at early, before finals rather than after.

GMB POST 2: ~9 August

Getting through footy finals 💪

Most soft-tissue footy injuries are preventable, and the players who finish the season standing up tend to do a few simple things well.

Before the game:
- Warm up properly. Ten minutes of rising-intensity running, leg swings and a few build-up sprints protects the hamstrings far more than static stretching
- Most soft-tissue injuries happen in the first quarter, on cold muscles, so the warm-up is not optional

Through the week:
- Respect the niggle. Tightness that has been there for weeks is information. Pushing through it is how a manageable strain becomes a full tear
- Load the calves and hamstrings. Calf raises and hamstring bridges a couple of times a week build the resilience heavy grounds demand
- Recover between games. Light movement, a proper cool-down and actual sleep do more than an ice bath

After a knock:
- A cork or a strain that you cannot run off is worth assessing early. Grading it properly is what separates a two-week injury from a two-month one

If something let go on the weekend and has not settled, get it looked at.
