## Why your shoulder hurts, and it's rarely just the shoulder

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and it pays for that freedom with stability. It leans on a cluster of small muscles, the rotator cuff, plus the shoulder blade sitting and gliding properly against the ribcage. When any of that stops working smoothly, the joint compensates, and the compensation is what starts to hurt.

Desk work sets the trap. Eight or nine hours hunched toward a monitor rounds the shoulders forward and lets the shoulder blades drift up and apart. Sit like that five days a week and the muscles at the front of the chest shorten while the ones between the shoulder blades switch off. The joint ends up sitting in a slightly worse position all day, every day.

Now add a Melbourne winter. Less daylight, colder mornings, training that quietly dropped off. Come spring you jump back into overhead pressing, a return to footy or cricket, or a big Saturday in the garden, and a shoulder that's been idle and poorly positioned gets asked to do explosive, loaded work. That mismatch, not a single bad rep, is what usually tips it over.

## What you can do about it today

You don't have to wait for an appointment to start helping the shoulder.

Break up the desk day. Every 30 to 40 minutes, roll your shoulders back and down and draw the shoulder blades gently together for a few seconds. It resets the position the joint sits in and stops the front of the chest tightening down.

Open the chest. Stand in a doorway, forearms on the frame, and lean through slowly until you feel a stretch across the front of the shoulders. Hold 30 seconds, twice. Desk work shortens these muscles, and lengthening them takes pressure off the joint.

Wake the shoulder blades up. Band pull-aparts or a simple wall slide, done daily, switch the mid-back muscles back on so they can support the joint instead of the neck doing the work.

Ramp back in slowly. If you're returning to the gym or sport, add load and overhead work gradually over a few weeks rather than picking up where you left off in autumn. The tissue needs time to catch up to the ambition.

If a movement causes sharp pain, weakness, or wakes you at night, stop pushing through it and get it looked at.

**What's the difference between an osteopath and a physio for shoulder pain?**
There's a lot of overlap, and both treat shoulders well. Osteopaths tend to take a whole-body view, looking at how your upper back, neck and shoulder blade feed into the problem, and use plenty of hands-on treatment of the joints and soft tissue alongside exercise. Physiotherapy often leans harder on structured exercise rehab. For a desk-driven, stiff, aching shoulder, the osteopathic approach of freeing the whole region and then rebuilding control suits a lot of people. The best choice usually comes down to the practitioner and your preference.

**How many sessions will I need for shoulder pain?**
It depends on how long it's been there and what's driving it. A recent flare from a spring training jump might settle in two or three sessions. A shoulder that's been niggling for months, tied to years of desk posture, usually takes longer, often four to six visits spread out, plus the home exercises doing their work between appointments. Most people feel a change after the first session. Your practitioner will give you a clearer estimate once they've assessed what's going on.

**Can shoulder problems cause neck pain or headaches?**
Yes, and it runs both ways. The neck, shoulder and upper back share muscles and nerves, so a shoulder that isn't moving well often loads the neck, which can bring on tension headaches. Equally, a stiff neck can refer pain down into the shoulder and mimic a shoulder injury. This is why a good assessment looks at the whole region rather than just the sore spot. Treating only where it hurts often misses the actual driver.

**Is shoulder pain from work covered by WorkCover?**
It can be, if the pain is linked to your work, for example a poor desk setup or a repetitive task. WorkCover claims need the right paperwork and usually a GP involved, so the process differs from a standard appointment. If you think your shoulder is work-related, mention it when you book and our reception can explain what's needed. For everyday shoulder pain not tied to a specific work injury, private health extras is the usual path.
