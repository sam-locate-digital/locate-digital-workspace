It's finals month across Melbourne, and not just at the MCG. Every suburban and country league, from the VAFA to your local Sunday competition, is down to its last few weekends, which means a lot of blokes who play for fun are being asked to do September things on a body that's carried them since April.

That's the part people underestimate. By the time finals arrive you've got twenty-odd weeks of training and matches in the legs, a niggle or two you've been quietly managing, and suddenly the games matter more, the tackles come in harder, and there's an edge to every contest. The body that got you through the home-and-away season isn't automatically the body that backs up through a finals campaign.

We see it every September at the Bourke Street and Essendon clinics. Not always dramatic injuries. More often it's players who've simply run out of road, stiff and sore and struggling to recover between games. Here's what's going on, and how to get through the run home in one piece.

## Why the end of the season is when your body gives out

Recovery is a physical process, and it runs on a budget. Every hard session and every game spends some of it; sleep, food and easy days top it back up. Across a long winter of footy, most weekend players quietly run a deficit. You never quite clear last week's soreness before you load it up again.

For most of the season you get away with it. Finals are where the bill lands. The intensity steps up, the matches are more physical, and the window between them gets shorter if you're playing more than one grade or backing up midweek. Meanwhile the small stuff you've ignored since June, the tight calf, the grumbly lower back, the hamstring that pulls up sore every Sunday, has had months to dig in.

Then there's the Melbourne context. You're at a desk five days a week, so between games you're not really resting, you're sitting, which leaves the hips tight and the back stiff before you even warm up. Age plays in too. Past 30, tissue takes longer to bounce back, and the recovery habits that worked at 22 stop cutting it. None of this is bad luck. It's an under-recovered body meeting the most demanding month of the year.

## Recovering well enough to back up

Most of what actually moves the needle on recovery is unglamorous and free.

Sleep first. It's the single biggest lever you have, and it's the one most weekend players skip. Seven to nine hours does more for tired legs than any recovery gadget on the market. If you play Saturday, guard Saturday and Sunday night above everything else.

Move on your day off rather than collapsing. A gentle walk, an easy spin on the bike, or a light swim the day after a game clears out more soreness than lying on the couch. Blood flow is what carries the recovery along, and sitting still all day does the opposite.

Eat and drink like it matters. Get some protein and carbohydrate in within an hour or two of the final siren, and keep the water up across the week, not just on game day. Under-fuelled muscle recovers slowly and cramps easily.

Warm up properly, especially in the cold. September mornings in Melbourne are still sharp, and cold muscle is stiff muscle. Give yourself a genuine ten to fifteen minutes before you go anywhere near top pace.

And respect the gap between sore and hurt. Muscle soreness that eases as you move is normal. Sharp pain, a sudden loss of power, or an ache that's worse the next morning is your body asking you to back off. Pushing through that in a final is how a niggle becomes a pre-season spent rehabbing.

## Melbourne in September: everyone's chasing one more week

There's a particular pressure to this time of year. The whole city is footy-mad through September, the AFL finals are on the telly, and at your own club the message is simple: win, or the season's over. Nobody wants to be the bloke who pulls out of a final, so players strap up, take something for the pain, and run out anyway.

Sometimes that's the right call and the body holds. Other times it turns a manageable strain into an injury that lingers into summer and wrecks your cricket or your running as well. If you're carrying something into a big game, it's worth a quick assessment beforehand rather than finding out the hard way at the first bounce. The clinics near Bourke Street and out in Essendon get busy with exactly this in September: players trying to squeeze one more good week out of a tired body.

When the season does finish, don't just stop and do nothing. The fortnight after your last game is the best window you'll get to finally deal with the niggles you've been carrying, so you start pre-season fresh instead of already behind.

## The takeaway

Finals footy is one of the better things about being a weekend player. The trick is remembering that the body carrying you through September has been at it since autumn, and it needs looking after to make it to the end. Recover hard, be honest about the difference between sore and injured, and get a proper look at anything that isn't right.

**Should I see an osteopath or a physio for a footy niggle?**
Both treat footy injuries well and there's plenty of overlap. Osteopaths tend to take a whole-body view, so rather than only treating the sore hamstring we'll look at how your hips, back and pelvis are moving and loading, since that's often what set it off. There's more hands-on treatment involved, which suits players who want to keep moving through a finals run. In practice the best choice is often whichever practitioner you trust and will actually keep seeing.

**How many sessions will I need to get through finals?**
It depends on what you're carrying. A single tight, grumbly area often settles enough to keep you playing after one or two visits, alongside the recovery work you do at home. Something that's been building all season, or a strain flaring under finals load, might need a bit more attention week to week until you're done. We'll be straight with you after the first look about whether it's safe to keep playing and what backing up will take.

**Can a tight lower back from footy cause other problems?**
Yes, and it's common. When the lower back and hips stiffen up, the load has to go somewhere, and it often shows up as a hamstring that keeps pulling up sore, a niggly groin, or knee pain. A back that isn't moving well also makes it harder to twist, kick and turn, which matters in a contact sport. It's one of the reasons we look at the whole area rather than just the spot that hurts, because the sore bit is often not the cause.

**Is a footy injury covered by private health?**
If you have extras cover that includes osteopathy, you can claim a rebate on the spot through HICAPS, and how much depends on your fund and level of cover. An injury from playing club footy generally isn't a WorkCover matter, since that covers work-related injury. Some clubs also have their own insurance or a sports trainer arrangement, so it's worth asking at the club too.
