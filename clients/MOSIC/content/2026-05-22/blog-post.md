## Why Cold Weather and Desk Work Are a Bad Combination

When temperatures drop, your body instinctively contracts muscles to conserve warmth. Your shoulders ride up toward your ears. Your chest tightens. Your upper trapezius, levator scapulae, and suboccipital muscles — the structures that suspend and stabilise your neck — enter a state of low-grade contraction that persists for hours.

Layer on top of that the typical posture of a Melbourne office worker: head forward, chin jutting, shoulders rounded over a laptop or dual monitors. This forward head position shifts the effective load of your skull from around 5 kilograms to the equivalent of 15–20 kilograms at the base of the neck. The muscles work overtime to hold it there. Through summer, this might cause occasional stiffness. In winter, with the additional muscular bracing against cold, it compounds into persistent pain.

The morning commute doesn't help. Walking from Flinders Street or Parliament station to an office on Bourke Street or Collins Street in a winter coat — shoulders hunched, chin tucked — does not warm your spine up. It reinforces the very pattern that the desk is about to exploit for the next eight hours.

For those who play AFL, run, cycle, or play tennis on weekends, there's another complication: tight cervical and thoracic muscles reduce rotational range of motion. You push through it during the game, but come Monday, the combination of effort and cold leaves the neck noticeably worse than when you started.

## What You Can Do Starting Today

You don't need a gym programme or expensive equipment to start reducing winter desk-driven neck pain. These practical habits make a real difference.

**Break the sitting cycle every 45 minutes.** Stand up, place your hands on your lower back, and gently extend through the thoracic spine. It takes sixty seconds and directly counters the flexion load that builds through the day.

**Warm up before your morning commute.** Three or four gentle chin tucks — slowly drawing your chin straight back while keeping your eyes level — and ten shoulder rolls before you leave the house prepares your neck for the cold walk to the station.

**Audit your remote work setup.** In winter, many CBD professionals from Carlton, Fitzroy, and South Yarra work from home more often — and on makeshift setups at kitchen tables or couches. A screen below eye height and no lumbar support for a full workday is a significant problem. Spend ten minutes adjusting your setup; it pays dividends immediately.

**Layer up during weekend sport.** A thermal base layer or neck gaiter for early morning runs or weekend footy means your upper back and neck start the session warm, reducing the risk of a cold-weather flare during or after the game.

**Treat a persistent stiff neck as a signal, not a minor inconvenience.** Two weeks of unresolved neck stiffness that began with winter sitting is unlikely to self-resolve. Getting it assessed early — rather than after it develops into headaches or referred arm symptoms — means faster recovery and less disruption to work and sport.
